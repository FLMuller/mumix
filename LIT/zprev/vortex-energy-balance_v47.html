Discrepancies met

  • The chord length of a hot wire. A probe records a straight cut through the tube, not the tube. At a random offset from the axis and a random tilt the spacing of the two extrema of the signal grows and the amplitude falls (A0c). Averaged over all tubes, Mouri and Hori (2009) read \(R_0=8.3\)–\(9.2\,\eta\) and \(V_0=2.4\)–\(3.2\,U_\eta\), against about 5 \(\eta\) and 4–10 \(U_\eta\) in the simulations; the authors say so themselves. The chord model of A0c brings the radius to 5.0 \(\eta\) (a divisor of 1.75) and gives a swirl factor between 2 and 3.5, 3 being used. The tails of the same experiments (the highest 0.1% and 1% of the velocity increments, Mouri et al. 2004, 2007) keep the near-axis crossings and read 5.2–7.4 \(\eta\) and 5–14 \(U_\eta\); they are left out of the fits.
  • The extreme swirl values. The swirl is the least consistent quantity. Ghira et al. (2022) list 6.1–10.4 \(U_\eta\) at \(Re_\lambda=90\)–400, above Kida et al. (3.7–3.9) and Da Silva et al. (3.7–4.3) at the same \(Re_\lambda\) and above the 5.5–6.2 that their own mean axis vorticity implies; a factor 0.6 puts them on the common curve. Mouri and Hori (2009) are too low (the chord) and the 0.1% class of Mouri et al. (2007) is too high for a whole tube. The swirl of Belin et al. (1996) needs the unknown tilt of the filament (a factor of about 3.6) and is not used. Above \(Re_\lambda\approx400\) the swirl rests on one source.
  • The definition of the radius. The 1/e radius of the vorticity is the Burgers radius, \(r_{\max}=1.12\,R_B\); Kida et al. (2002) and Aligolzadeh et al. (2023) give a core boundary (a factor 0.6 and a value 6.0 \(\eta\) that is not corrected); Ishihara et al. and Sassa and Makita give a diameter of order \(10\,\eta\) without a mean.
  • The volume of the tubes. Intense tubes are 1% of the volume by definition; low-pressure cores take 31–35% (Kida et al. 2002). The mean spacing \(d/\eta\) is given only by Kida et al., so the volume fraction \(V_f\) of the dissipation budget has one check.

The aim of this work is to construct a subgrid closure model based on physical and numerical experiments. The tube is a Burgers vortex whose size, swirl and strain, written in Kolmogorov units, depend on \(Re_\lambda\) only; sections 3–8 derive its energy budget, section 9 collects the data and sections 10 and 11 fit the radius, swirl and circulation as functions of \(Re_\lambda\).

2. Theory background

Vortex stretching

In an incompressible flow the vorticity \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u\) follows

$$\frac{D\boldsymbol\omega}{Dt}=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u+\nu\nabla^2\boldsymbol\omega .$$

The first term on the right is vortex stretching: a strain rate \(\gamma\) along the vorticity raises \(|\boldsymbol\omega|\) at the rate \(\gamma\) and, because the circulation is conserved (Kelvin), thins the vortex. The last term is viscous diffusion, which spreads the vorticity at the rate \(\nu/r^2\). Small scales are made by stretching and removed by viscosity; in turbulence the vorticity lies preferentially along the intermediate eigenvector of the strain, which is mostly extensional (Ashurst et al. 1987), so the stretching is persistent.

The Burgers vortex

Burgers (1948) found the exact steady solution in which the two effects balance: a vortex of circulation \(\Gamma\) in the axisymmetric strain \(\mathbf u=(-\gamma r/2,\,0,\,\gamma z)\), whose radial inflow convects the vorticity inwards as fast as viscosity spreads it out,

$$u_\theta=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}\left(1-e^{-\gamma r^2/4\nu}\right),\qquad \omega_z=\frac{\Gamma\gamma}{4\pi\nu}\,e^{-\gamma r^2/4\nu}.$$

Its size, \(r_{\max}=2.24\sqrt{\nu/\gamma}\) (section 3), depends on the strain and the viscosity and not on the circulation; its Reynolds numbers are \(Re_\gamma=\gamma r_{\max}^2/\nu=4x=5.03\) (fixed) and \(Re_c=r_{\max}U_{\max}/\nu\) or \(\Gamma/\nu=8.78\,Re_c\) (free). The dissipation it adds to the strain, \(\rho\gamma\Gamma^2/8\pi\) per unit length, does not depend on \(\nu\) (section 3). The vortex is a model: the strain is imposed and does not feel the vortex.

Stretched vortices in turbulence

Townsend (1951) described the fine structure of turbulence as a collection of Burgers-type vortex sheets and tubes. Tennekes (1968) built the small scales from a random array of Burgers vortices and derived their spectrum and size. Lundgren (1982) wound a vortex sheet into a spiral round a stretched tube (the strained spiral vortex), which gives the \(k^{-5/3}\) spectrum, and Horiuti and Fujisawa (2008) extended the idea to several modes in a simulation. Robinson and Saffman (1984) and Neu (1984) studied how a stretched vortex is changed by a non-axisymmetric strain and by its own induced flow. Moffatt, Kida and Ohkitani (1994) showed for large Reynolds number \(\Gamma/\nu\) that the stretched vortex is an asymptotic solution of the Navier–Stokes equations when the strain is not axisymmetric, called such vortices the sinews of turbulence, and found that the excess dissipation of a Burgers vortex does not change in a non-axisymmetric strain while 98.7% of it falls outside \(r=\sqrt2\,\delta\), where only 37% of the enstrophy sits (section 3). The tube is thus the part of the structure that carries the vorticity, and most of the dissipation it causes lies in its shear layer.

Scales and what the Burgers vortex says about them

Richardson (1922) pictured the energy passing from large eddies to smaller ones, and Kolmogorov (1941) gave the scales at which it ends, set by \(\nu\) and the dissipation \(\varepsilon\):

$$\eta=\left(\frac{\nu^3}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4},\qquad U_\eta=(\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4},\qquad \gamma_\eta=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}\right)^{1/2},\qquad Re_\eta=\frac{U_\eta\eta}{\nu}=1 .$$

Dissipation is intermittent (Meneveau and Sreenivasan 1991) and the velocity-gradient field is organised in vortex tubes and sheets (Nomura and Post 1998). Write the Burgers vortex in Kolmogorov units, \(r_{\max}=\beta\eta\) and \(U_{\max}=u\,U_\eta\). Then \(\gamma r_{\max}^2=4x\nu\) gives

$$\beta^2\,\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_\eta}=4x=5.03,\qquad Re_c=\beta\,u,\qquad \frac{\Gamma}{\nu}=8.78\,\beta u .$$

By dimensional analysis the two numbers \(\beta\) and \(u\) depend on \(Re_\lambda\) only, and nothing else about the tube is free. This is what sections 9–11 determine from the data. A radius of \(5\eta\) means a strain \(\gamma=0.2\,\gamma_\eta\): the strain that stretches the tubes is a fixed fraction of the Kolmogorov strain at every \(Re_\lambda\). One reading is that where the strain exceeds the Kolmogorov value eddies form and reduce it, so that the strain that survives around a tube is a fraction of \(\gamma_\eta\); the radius and the strain are then one observation, not two.

Simulations and experiments

The tubes were found in simulations as connected regions of high vorticity, the worms of Jiménez et al. (1993) and Jiménez and Wray (1998), and later as the intense vorticity structures (the 1% of the volume of highest vorticity) whose radius is the 1/e radius of the vorticity (Da Silva et al. 2011; Ghira et al. 2022, 2025; Moisy and Jiménez 2004 on their geometry and clustering). Other definitions give more of the volume: low-pressure vortex cores (Kida et al. 2002; Makihara et al. 2002; 31–35% of the volume), coherent fine-scale eddies from the second invariant of the velocity gradient (Tanahashi et al. 2001, 2004), cores scanned out of a database without a threshold (Pirozzoli 2009, 2012) and vortex tubes in thin shear layers (Ishihara et al. 2011, 2013; Elsinga et al. 2023). In experiments a tube is a conditional average of the signal around events of a large velocity increment, fitted with a Burgers profile (Mouri et al. 2003, 2004, 2007; Mouri and Hori 2009; Sassa and Makita 2005), or the tails of the distribution of a velocity derivative (Belin et al. 1996), or an objectively defined vortex in a 3D measurement (Aligolzadeh et al. 2023). The radius is about 5 \(\eta\) in all of them once the geometry of the cut and the definition are accounted for (section 9); the swirl and the circulation differ because the selection decides which tubes are counted (section 9).

3. One stretched vortex

Velocity profile and core size

With viscous core length \(\delta=\sqrt{\nu/\gamma}\), the azimuthal velocity is

$$U_\theta(R)=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi R}\left(1-e^{-\gamma R^2/4\nu}\right).$$

Put \(x=\gamma R^2/4\nu\). The velocity peaks where

$$e^{x}-2x-1=0\;\Rightarrow\;x=1.2564,\qquad r_{\max}=\sqrt{4x}\,\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\gamma}}=2.242\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\gamma}}.$$ $$U_{\max}=\frac{1-e^{-x}}{2\pi}\,\frac{\Gamma}{r_{\max}}=0.1138\,\frac{\Gamma}{r_{\max}}=0.0508\,\Gamma\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\nu}},\qquad \Gamma=8.78\,U_{\max}\,r_{\max}.$$

Dissipation of the whole structure

The background strain is irrotational, so integrating the enstrophy isolates the excess caused by the vortex (Burgers eq. 60, Moffatt eq. 3.36):

$$P_{\text{ex}}=\rho\nu\int_0^\infty \omega^2\,2\pi r\,dr=\frac{\rho A C^2}{4\pi}=\frac{\rho\,\gamma\,\Gamma^2}{8\pi}\quad\text{per unit length.}$$

It does not depend on \(\nu\). Moffatt's addition is that non-axisymmetric strain leaves this total unchanged while 98.7% of it falls outside \(r=\sqrt2\,\delta\), where only 37% of the enstrophy sits.

4. Dissipation as flow times pressure drop

The radial inflow through the cylinder \(r=r_{\max}\), per unit length, uses \(\alpha+\beta=-\gamma\):

$$Q=\pi r_{\max}^2\,\gamma .$$

Dividing the dissipation by \(Q\) cancels \(\gamma\):

$$\frac{P_{\text{ex}}}{Q}=\frac{\rho\,\Gamma^2}{8\pi^2 r_{\max}^2}=\frac{\rho\,U_{\max}^2}{2\,(1-e^{-x})^2}=0.977\,\rho\,U_{\max}^2=1.954\cdot\tfrac12\rho U_{\max}^2 .$$

\(P_{\text{ex}}=Q\,\Delta P\) with \(\Delta P\approx\rho U_{\max}^2\), matching the core pressure drop Burgers quotes. The same constant holds for Moffatt's non-axisymmetric strain.

There is no excess axial velocity: the vortex induces only cross-flow motion and \(U_z=\gamma z\) is the background. The "axial energy flux" branch in the session (constants 3.73, 0.244, 0.593, 0.379) was withdrawn by Gemini and should be dropped.

5. Background strain dissipation

For \(U=(\alpha x,\beta y,\gamma z)\) with \(\alpha=-\tfrac12(1+\lambda)\gamma\), \(\beta=-\tfrac12(1-\lambda)\gamma\):

$$\varepsilon_{\text{strain}}=2\nu\left(\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2\right)=\nu\gamma^2\left(\lambda^2+3\right)\;\xrightarrow{\;\lambda=0\;}\;3\nu\gamma^2 .$$

6. Global budget

\(Q\) is per metre of vortex (m²/s) and \(L_e\) is the vortex length per unit volume (m/m³), so \(L_eQ\) is the volume drawn into the cores per second per unit volume, in 1/s. The budget fixes it:

$$\varepsilon-3\nu\gamma^2=0.977\,(L_eQ)\,U_{\max}^2\quad\Rightarrow\quad L_eQ=\frac{\varepsilon-3\nu\gamma^2}{0.977\,U_{\max}^2}.$$

The core volume fraction is then an output, not an input. Derivation: each metre of tube occupies the cross-section \(\pi r_{\max}^2\) (m³ per m of tube), and there are \(L_e\) metres of tube per m³ of fluid, so the fraction of volume inside the cores is

$$V_f=L_e\,\pi r_{\max}^2 .$$

The inflow per metre of tube is \(Q=\pi\gamma r_{\max}^2\) (section 4), so \(\pi r_{\max}^2=Q/\gamma\) and

$$V_f=\frac{L_eQ}{\gamma}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L_eQ=V_f\,\gamma .$$

Meaning: the cores hold a fraction \(V_f\) of the volume, and the strain field refills that volume at the rate \(\gamma\), so the volume drawn in per second per unit volume is \(V_f\gamma\). Because \(V_f\) is a fraction of the cylinder's own cross-section, it is a cross-section of the core, not of the whole dissipative eddy. The dissipation spreads over a larger radius than \(r_{\max}\) (98.7% of it lies outside \(r=\sqrt2\,\delta\)); that is a limit of this \(Q\,\Delta P\) bookkeeping.

Correction to the session. Gemini assumed \(V_f=1\%\) as an input and reduced \(L_eQ\) to \(0.0503\,\nu\). That value has units m²/s, because it cancelled \(\pi r_{\max}^2\) against a \(Q\) already evaluated as \(5.03\,\nu\) (which also dropped a factor \(\pi\)). It then discarded the mismatched term as "negligible". The 22.8% eddy share it reported came from that slip. With the units kept, \(L_eQ=V_f\gamma\) and everything is consistent.

7. Closing with Kolmogorov scales

$$\eta=\left(\frac{\nu^3}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4},\quad U_\eta=(\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4},\quad \gamma_\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}},\quad U_\eta\,\eta=\nu .$$

Assumption: the tube's peak-swirl radius and peak velocity scale with the Kolmogorov scales through one dial \(\beta\),

$$r_{\max}=\beta\,\eta,\qquad U_{\max}=\beta\,U_\eta .$$

Fixing the two separately would overdetermine the problem, which caused the repeated loops in the session. Here \(\beta\) is just \(r_{\max}/\eta\), the tube radius in Kolmogorov lengths (the core diameter is \(2\beta\eta\)).

Step 1: what \(x\) is and where it comes from

\(x\) is the dimensionless radius squared of the Burgers vortex,

$$x(R)=\frac{\gamma R^2}{4\nu}=\left(\frac{R}{2\delta}\right)^2,\qquad \delta=\sqrt{\nu/\gamma}.$$

Substituting \(R^2=4\nu x/\gamma\) into \(U_\theta=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi R}\left(1-e^{-x}\right)\) gives the velocity as a function of \(x\) alone:

$$U_\theta=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\nu}}\;\frac{1-e^{-x}}{\sqrt{x}} .$$

The peak is where \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dx\left[(1-e^{-x})\,x^{-1/2}\right]=0\):

$$e^{-x}x^{-1/2}-\tfrac12(1-e^{-x})x^{-3/2}=0\;\Rightarrow\;2x\,e^{-x}=1-e^{-x}\;\Rightarrow\;e^{x}-2x-1=0 .$$

This has the single positive root (found by Newton iteration) $$\boxed{\,x=1.25643\,},\qquad e^{x}=3.51286=2x+1,\qquad 1-e^{-x}=0.71533 .$$ So \(x\) is a pure number fixed by the shape of the profile. It converts between the peak radius and the viscous scale:

$$r_{\max}^2=\frac{4x\,\nu}{\gamma}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\gamma=\frac{4x\,\nu}{r_{\max}^2}.$$

Step 2: strain rate from \(r_{\max}=\beta\eta\)

The Kolmogorov scales satisfy \(\eta^2=\nu/\gamma_\eta\) with \(\gamma_\eta=\sqrt{\varepsilon/\nu}\). Then \(r_{\max}^2=\beta^2\nu/\gamma_\eta\), and

$$\gamma=\frac{4x\,\nu}{\beta^2\nu/\gamma_\eta}=\frac{4x}{\beta^2}\,\gamma_\eta .$$

Step 3: circulation from \(U_{\max}=\beta U_\eta\)

The peak velocity in terms of \(\Gamma\) and \(r_{\max}\) is \(U_{\max}=\frac{(1-e^{-x})\,\Gamma}{2\pi r_{\max}}\). Solving for \(\Gamma\) and inserting both conditions together with \(U_\eta\eta=\nu\):

$$\Gamma=\frac{2\pi\,r_{\max}U_{\max}}{1-e^{-x}}=\frac{2\pi\,(\beta\eta)(\beta U_\eta)}{1-e^{-x}}=\frac{2\pi}{1-e^{-x}}\,\beta^2\,\nu=8.78\,\beta^2\,\nu .$$

Step 4: the Reynolds numbers

Three Reynolds numbers appear. Each is a velocity times a length over \(\nu\).

\(R_\Gamma\) is the inverse of Moffatt's small parameter, \(\epsilon=\nu/\Gamma=1/R_\Gamma\), so the asymptotic theory needs \(R_\Gamma\gg1\). The core Reynolds number follows from the \(U_{\max}\) relation above:

$$Re_c=\frac{U_{\max}r_{\max}}{\nu}=\frac{\beta U_\eta\cdot\beta\eta}{\nu}=\beta^2,\qquad R_\Gamma=\frac{2\pi}{1-e^{-x}}\,Re_c=8.78\,Re_c=8.78\,\beta^2 .$$

The strain Reynolds number is just Step 1 rearranged, so it is the same for every Burgers vortex: \(Re_\gamma=\gamma r_{\max}^2/\nu=4x\). For comparison, a structure at exactly the Kolmogorov scale has Reynolds number \(U_\eta\eta/\nu=1\), which \(Re_c=\beta^2\) reaches at \(\beta=1\).

Summary:

$$\gamma=\frac{4x\,\nu}{r_{\max}^2}=\frac{4x}{\beta^2}\,\gamma_\eta,\qquad \Gamma=\frac{2\pi\,r_{\max}U_{\max}}{1-e^{-x}}=8.78\,\beta^2\,\nu,\qquad R_\Gamma=\frac{\Gamma}{\nu}=8.78\,\beta^2 .$$

Step 5: strain share of the budget

Start from the background dissipation of section 5, \(\varepsilon_{\text{strain}}=3\nu\gamma^2\), and substitute \(\gamma\) from Step 2:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{strain}}=3\nu\left(\frac{4x}{\beta^2}\gamma_\eta\right)^2=\frac{48\,x^2}{\beta^4}\;\nu\gamma_\eta^2 .$$

Now \(\nu\gamma_\eta^2=\nu\cdot\varepsilon/\nu=\varepsilon\), so

$$\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{strain}}}{\varepsilon}=\frac{48\,x^2}{\beta^4}=\frac{75.8}{\beta^4}\qquad(48x^2=75.77).$$

Step 6: vortex share of the budget

Start from the vortex term of the section 6 budget, \(\varepsilon_{\text{vortex}}=0.977\,(L_eQ)\,U_{\max}^2\), where \(0.977=1/\left(2(1-e^{-x})^2\right)\) comes from section 4. Substitute in turn \(L_eQ=V_f\gamma\) (section 6), then \(\gamma=\frac{4x}{\beta^2}\gamma_\eta\) (Step 2) and \(U_{\max}^2=\beta^2U_\eta^2\):

$$\varepsilon_{\text{vortex}}=0.977\,V_f\,\gamma\,U_{\max}^2=0.977\,V_f\,\frac{4x}{\beta^2}\gamma_\eta\;\beta^2U_\eta^2=0.977\cdot4x\;V_f\;\gamma_\eta U_\eta^2 .$$

The factors \(\beta^2\) cancel, which is what the choice \(U_{\max}=\beta U_\eta\) buys. Next \(\gamma_\eta U_\eta^2=\sqrt{\varepsilon/\nu}\,\sqrt{\nu\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\), so

$$\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{vortex}}}{\varepsilon}=0.977\cdot4x\;V_f=4.91\,V_f .$$

Step 7: closing the budget and the volume fraction

The imposed dissipation is split between the background strain and the vortices, \(\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\text{strain}}+\varepsilon_{\text{vortex}}\). Dividing by \(\varepsilon\) and inserting Steps 5 and 6:

$$1=\frac{75.8}{\beta^4}+4.91\,V_f .$$

Solving for the volume fraction gives \(V_f\) as a function of the one dial \(\beta\):

$$\boxed{\;V_f=\frac{1-75.8/\beta^{4}}{4.91},\qquad r_{\max}=\beta\eta\;}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \beta=\left(\frac{75.8}{1-4.91\,V_f}\right)^{1/4}.$$

\(V_f\) must be positive, which needs \(\beta>75.8^{1/4}=2.95\), and cannot exceed \(1/4.91=20\%\) (the vortices would then carry all of \(\varepsilon\)).

Two more quantities complete the table. The inflow per metre of tube is the same for every \(\beta\):

$$Q=\pi\gamma r_{\max}^2=\pi\cdot4x\,\nu=15.8\,\nu ,$$

since \(\gamma r_{\max}^2=4x\nu\) (Step 1). The strain rate (small \(\gamma\)) and circulation (big \(\Gamma\)) follow from the summary of Step 4. Vortex length and spacing

The tube length per unit volume follows from \(L_e=V_f\gamma/Q\). At 1%: \(L_e=0.01\cdot178/1.58\times10^{-5}=1.1\times10^{5}\) m/m³, about 110 m of vortex per litre.

\(L_e\) has units m/m³ = 1/m², so each tube has on average \(1/L_e\) m² of cross-section to itself. For tubes roughly parallel and evenly spread, the mean spacing between axes is

$$d=\sqrt{\frac{V}{L}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_e}}\quad(L=L_eV\text{ metres of tube in a volume }V),$$

and with \(L_e=V_f/(\pi r_{\max}^2)\) from section 6:

$$d=r_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{V_f}}=\beta\,\eta\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{V_f}} .$$

So \(d/\eta\) depends on \(\beta\) and \(V_f\) only, not on \(\varepsilon\) or \(\nu\). For random orientation the spacing is of the same order; the exact factor depends on the orientation distribution.

8. A second closure: circulation set by the tube spacing

The budget leaves \(\beta\) free. A second relation comes from taking the circulation as the velocity difference across the mean spacing \(d\) (section 7) times that spacing, with the inertial-range velocity \(U(d)=(\varepsilon d)^{1/3}\):

$$\Gamma=c\,U(d)\,d=c\,\varepsilon^{1/3}d^{4/3}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{\Gamma}{\nu}=c\left(\frac{d}{\eta}\right)^{4/3},$$

using \(\varepsilon^{1/3}\eta^{4/3}=\nu\). Insert \(\Gamma/\nu=8.78\,\beta^2\) (Step 4) and \(d/\eta=\beta\sqrt{\pi/V_f}\) (spacing section):

$$8.78\,\beta^2=c\,\beta^{4/3}\left(\frac{\pi}{V_f}\right)^{2/3}\;\Rightarrow\;\beta=\left(\frac{c}{8.78}\right)^{3/2}\frac{\pi}{V_f}.$$

Together with the budget \(1=75.8/\beta^4+4.91\,V_f\) this has one root \(V_f\) for each \(c\).

Letting \(c\) depend on \(\varepsilon\)

\(c\) is dimensionless, so it can depend on \(\varepsilon\) only through a Reynolds number; at fixed large scale \(L\) and \(\nu\) that number is \(\propto\varepsilon^{1/3}\). Try

$$c=c_0\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0}\right)^{1/3},\qquad c_0=0.71\text{ at }\varepsilon_0=0.1\ \text{W/kg}.$$

The reference \(c_0=0.71\) is an assumed value, not fitted to data; it gives a vortex share of about 12% at 0.1 W/kg, in the range Moffatt quotes. Results for \(\nu=10^{-6}\) m²/s:

9. The data: whole tubes and intense tubes

The literature values of tube radius, swirl and circulation do not describe one population. Papers select tubes in different ways, and the selection changes the swirl and the circulation strongly and the radius only a little. Two sets are therefore distinguished, and every value in the appendix is assigned to one of them.

What is meant by the two sets

  • Intense tubes are the strongest structures only. They are the 1% of the volume with the highest vorticity (the intense vorticity structures or worms of Jiménez et al., Ghira et al., Da Silva et al.; 1.4% in Aligolzadeh et al.), so in the simulations and the 3D measurement they occupy a volume fraction \(V_i\approx0.01\). In the hot-wire records (Mouri et al. 2003, 2004, 2007) the cut is on the signal: the highest 0.1% or 1% of the absolute velocity increments, a share of the samples along the record and not of the volume. The volume that these tubes occupy is not given, so \(V_i\approx0.01\) applies to the DNS-defined tubes only.
  • Whole tubes are tube-like vortices selected with a low threshold or none: the low-pressure cores of Kida et al. (31–35% of the volume), the vortices fitted by Mouri and Hori (2009), the coherent fine-scale eddies of Tanahashi et al., the cores of Pirozzoli and the elementary vortices of Sassa and Makita. They occupy a volume fraction \(V_w\approx0.2\)–0.35, the order of the \(V_f\) of the budget in sections 6 and 7.

The intense tubes are the tail of the distribution of tube strengths and the whole set contains them. The two sets overlap in radius and differ in swirl. The ranges of the values used (rows with an \(Re_\lambda\); the number of values in brackets) are:

How the two sets scale

As reported, the swirl of intense tubes follows the r.m.s. velocity: \(V_0/\langle v^2\rangle^{1/2}\approx0.5\)–0.8 (Mouri et al.), \(u_0/u'\approx0.7\)–0.8 (Da Silva et al.) and \(U_{ivs}/u'\approx1.0\)–1.25 (Ghira et al.), so, with \(u'/U_\eta=(Re_\lambda/\sqrt{15})^{1/2}\) and a nearly constant radius \(\beta_i\approx5\),

$$U_i=\kappa\,u'=\kappa\left(\frac{Re_\lambda}{\sqrt{15}}\right)^{1/2}U_\eta,\qquad Re_{c,i}=\beta_iU_i/U_\eta\propto Re_\lambda^{1/2},\qquad \frac{\Gamma_i}{\nu}=8.78\,Re_{c,i}.$$

This agrees with the measured \(Re_0\propto Re_\lambda^{1/2}\) of Mouri et al. For whole tubes the swirl is a few Kolmogorov velocities and grows slowly (\(Re_0\propto Re_\lambda^{0.18}\) of Mouri and Hori, \(\Gamma/\nu\propto Re_\lambda^{0.16}\) of Kida et al., \(W_{\max}/u_K\propto Re_\lambda^{0.28}\) of Sassa and Makita). The two behaviours are two sets of numbers, not necessarily two kinds of tube: a hot wire reads a chord of the tube and the threshold decides which chords are kept (A0c). The corrected values of the whole tubes (Mouri and Hori × 3) and of the intense simulations (Ghira et al. × 0.6) fall on one curve, \(U_{\max}/U_\eta\approx0.8\,Re_\lambda^{1/3}\) (section 10), and a fit with a separate swirl law for each set (T6 of section 10) gives two nearly identical curves.

This is one simulation (a mixing layer at one Reynolds number) and the figure was digitised by eye: the numbers carry an uncertainty of about 10%, and the far tail beyond \(D^*\approx60\) a factor of 2–3.

How this enters the model

The dissipation budget of sections 6 and 7 describes the whole set: the volume \(V_f\) it closes is the volume of the tubes that carry the vortex dissipation, and the swirl in it must be that of the whole set,

$$\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{vortex}}}{\varepsilon}=4.91\,V_w\,\frac{(U_w/U_\eta)^2}{\beta_w^{2}}\;\Rightarrow\;V_w=\frac{\left(1-75.8/\beta_w^{4}\right)\beta_w^{2}}{4.91\,(U_w/U_\eta)^{2}}.$$

The intense tubes are a small subset, with a volume \(V_i\approx0.01\) and a share of the dissipation

$$\frac{\varepsilon_i}{\varepsilon}=4.91\,V_i\,\frac{(U_i/U_\eta)^2}{\beta_i^{2}}.$$

With \(V_i=0.01\), \(\beta_i=5\) and \(U_i/U_\eta=9\) (\(Re_\lambda\approx300\)) this is \(\varepsilon_i/\varepsilon\approx0.16\), close to the 15% of the enstrophy that Jiménez et al. find in their intense 1%. So the two sets are consistent with each other in the budget, but they must not be fitted by the same swirl law.

The fits of section 10 use the corrected values of both sets together, every source weighted equally within each quantity (radius, swirl, circulation). The values fitted are those marked "fitted" in the appendix. The hot-wire tails of Mouri et al. (2004, 2007) are left out: they come from the same experiments as Mouri and Hori (2009), and a high threshold keeps the near-axis crossings of the same tubes (A0c).

10. The fits tried

Every law below was fitted to the corrected data of section 9 by the fitting tool (LIT/fittingtool; the report of each run is a self-contained page, results/fit_*.html). The data are the values used in the table of section 9 with a single \(Re_\lambda\): 32 radii and 22 swirls from 10 sources (the hot-wire tails of Mouri et al. 2004 and 2007 are left out, A0c); the circulation is \(\Gamma/\nu=8.78\,r_{\max}U_{\max}/(\eta U_\eta)\) from the corrected values, as in section 9. Every source counts equally within a quantity (the weights of its points sum to the same total), so a source with one point counts as much as a source with twenty. The fit minimises the sum of the squares of \(\ln(\text{model}/\text{data})\) over the quantities the model is fitted to.

The scores are the same for every model, whatever it was fitted to: the weighted rms of \(\ln(\text{model}/\text{data})\) for the radius, the swirl and the circulation (a score of 8% means the model is typically 8% from the data), and the combined score \(\sqrt{(\text{rms}_r^2+\text{rms}_U^2)/2}\), which ranks the models. The parameter errors are from the Jacobian of the fit with every point treated as independent; they are too small, because points of one source and points of one flow are correlated.

The equations are grouped by what the model takes as given. \(U_\eta\) is the Kolmogorov velocity, \(u'/U_\eta=(Re_\lambda/\sqrt{15})^{1/2}\), \(8.78=2\pi/(1-e^{-x})\) with \(x=1.2564\) (section 3), \(\ln\) the natural logarithm.

What the fits say

  • The radius is constant. A constant radius fits to \(r_{\max}=5.02\,\eta\) (D2). Letting it vary as a power of \(Re_\lambda\) gives an exponent \(m=6.51e-05 ± 0.015\) (D3) and as a straight line in \(\ln Re_\lambda\) a slope 0.00033 ± 0.077 per unit \(\ln Re_\lambda\) (D7), both consistent with zero, and the score does not improve. The radius 5 (D1) is within one standard error of the fitted constant.
  • The swirl grows as a power of \(Re_\lambda\) close to one third. D1 gives \(U_{\max}/U_\eta=0.838\,Re_\lambda^{0.326}\), D6 fixes the exponent at 1/3 and gives \(0.807\,Re_\lambda^{1/3}\) with the same score. A straight line in \(\ln Re_\lambda\) (L1) is slightly worse (8.5% against 8.2%) and the swirl proportional to \(u'\) (D4, exponent 1/2) is clearly worse (14.1%). The fit of the circulation as a power law (C1, \(Q=0.305\)) is as good as the fit of the swirl.
  • The closures do not help. K3 (swirl a power of the radius) and K5 (a log law of the swirl with a power law of the circulation) make the radius vary with \(Re_\lambda\), against the data (scores 17.8% and 10.2%). K4 reduces to a constant radius (\(q=0\), \(r_{\max}=4.9\,\eta\)) and a power-law swirl.
  • The two tube sets are not different. With a separate swirl law for each (T6), the intense tubes give \(0.701\,Re_\lambda^{0.365}\) and the whole tubes \(0.805\,Re_\lambda^{0.33}\), nearly the same curve, with only a slightly better score (8.0%) for two more parameters; this model is information only.

The scores of D1, D2, D3, D6, D7 and D8 differ by less than 0.1 percentage points: the data fix the radius (5) and the swirl (a power near 1/3), and nothing more. The best combined score of a model that is not information only is 8.2%.

11. The selected model

The simplest model whose combined score is within 25% of the best is D6, with one parameter. The tube is a Burgers vortex with

$$\boxed{\;\frac{r_{\max}}{\eta}=5,\qquad \frac{U_{\max}}{U_\eta}=c\,Re_\lambda^{1/3},\qquad c=0.807\pm0.014\;}$$

and everything else follows from the relations of sections 3–7. The error on \(c\) is from the fit with independent points (too small); the free exponent of D1 is \(0.326\pm0.019\) and the fitted constant radius of D2 is \(5.02\pm0.07\,\eta\). The values of \(c\) and the exponent depend on the factor applied to Mouri and Hori (2009), section 10.

What follows from the two lines

With \(r_{\max}=\beta\eta\), \(\beta=5\) (section 7):

$$\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_\eta}=\frac{4x}{\beta^2}=0.201,\qquad Re_\gamma=\frac{\gamma r_{\max}^2}{\nu}=4x=5.03,\qquad Q=\pi\gamma r_{\max}^2=15.8\,\nu,$$ $$Re_c=\frac{r_{\max}U_{\max}}{\nu}=\beta\frac{U_{\max}}{U_\eta}=4.04\,Re_\lambda^{1/3},\qquad \frac{\Gamma}{\nu}=8.78\,Re_c=35.4\,Re_\lambda^{1/3}.$$

The strain rate that stretches the tube is a fixed fraction of the Kolmogorov strain, \(\gamma\approx0.2\,\gamma_\eta\): the radius 5 and the strain 1/5 are one observation (\(\beta^2\gamma/\gamma_\eta=4x\) is fixed by the Burgers vortex). The swirl grows as \(Re_\lambda^{1/3}\) while the radius does not, so the circulation of the tube grows as \(Re_\lambda^{1/3}\) in units of \(\nu\), and the tube becomes relatively stronger at higher \(Re_\lambda\) (\(Re_c\) from 15 at \(Re_\lambda=50\) to 51 at 2000).

The dissipation budget

The dissipation inside the tubes enters through the vortex term of section 6, \(\varepsilon_{\text{vortex}}/\varepsilon=4.91\,V_f\,(U_{\max}/(\beta U_\eta))^2\), where \(V_f\) is the fraction of the volume inside the cores (\(r $$\frac{f_v}{V_f}=4.91\left(\frac{U_{\max}}{\beta U_\eta}\right)^2=0.128\,Re_\lambda^{2/3}:$$

the dissipation per unit volume inside the cores is 2.8 times the mean at \(Re_\lambda=100\) and 20 times at 2000. This is the closure-free result of the fits.

The closure of section 6, \(\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\text{strain}}+\varepsilon_{\text{vortex}}\) with \(\varepsilon_{\text{strain}}=3\nu\gamma^2\) at the strain of the tubes (\(0.2\gamma_\eta\)), adds a second relation: the strain takes 12% of \(\varepsilon\) at \(\beta=5\) at every \(Re_\lambda\), the tubes the other 88%, and \(V_f\) follows (the last two columns of the table; the spacing is \(d=r_{\max}\sqrt{\pi/V_f}\)). It is an assumption, not a result: it takes the dissipation outside the tubes to be that of the strain that feeds the tubes. In the picture where eddies form when the strain exceeds \(\gamma_\eta\) and limit it, as the radius 5 suggests, this is consistent. The one measurement that tests it is the mean tube spacing of Kida et al. (2002), \(d/\eta=15.6\) and 17.2 at \(Re_\lambda=83\) and 170, which gives \(V_f=\pi r_{\max}^2/d^2=0.35\) and 0.31, against 0.36 and 0.22 from the closure: it agrees at 83 and is 40% higher at 170. The volume fraction of the closure falls from 0.5 at \(Re_\lambda=50\) to 0.04 at 2000 and its share is constant (0.88) because the radius is constant.

Limits

  • The exponent 1/3 and the factor 3 for Mouri and Hori (2009) are linked: the data allow a factor between 2.4 and 3 (free exponent 0.24–0.33), and a larger factor, with Ghira et al. as listed, moves the exponent to about 0.5 at a much worse score (section 10). The chord model of A0c gives 2–3.5 for the factor. The swirl above \(Re_\lambda\approx400\) rests on one source.
  • The radius 5 is the Burgers radius of the swirl maximum; sources that give a boundary radius or a 1/e radius are converted with the Burgers relations (A0). The PIV radius of Aligolzadeh et al. (6.0 \(\eta\)) is a boundary radius and is not corrected.
  • The budget quantities (\(V_f\), the share and the spacing) depend on the closure above, and \(V_f\) is only checked at two points.
  • The tubes are straight Burgers vortices; their lengths, curvature, clustering and lifetime (Ghira et al. 2025) are not part of the model.

This is the tube that the subgrid closure of the next step has to represent: a vortex of radius \(5\eta\), swirl \(0.81\,Re_\lambda^{1/3}U_\eta\) and strain \(0.2\gamma_\eta\) at every \(Re_\lambda\) from 35 to 1900.

A0. Conversions used for all sources

A Burgers vortex has \(u_\theta(r)=\dfrac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}\left(1-e^{-r^2/R_B^2}\right)\) with \(R_B=2\sqrt{\nu/\gamma}\), the radius at which the vorticity has fallen to \(1/e\) of its axis value. The swirl is maximal at \(r_{\max}=\sqrt{x}\,R_B=1.121\,R_B\), where \(x=1.2564\) is the root of \(e^x-2x-1=0\), and

$$U_{\max}=\frac{(1-e^{-x})\,\Gamma}{2\pi\,r_{\max}},\qquad \frac{\Gamma}{\nu}=\frac{2\pi}{1-e^{-x}}\,\frac{r_{\max}U_{\max}}{\nu}=8.78\,Re_c,\qquad Re_c=\frac{r_{\max}U_{\max}}{\nu}.$$

The circulation inside a radius \(r\) is \(\Gamma(r)=\Gamma\left(1-e^{-r^2/R_B^2}\right)\) and the swirl at \(r=k\,r_{\max}\) is \(U_{\max}f(k)\), with

$$f(k)=\frac{1-e^{-xk^2}}{k\,(1-e^{-x})}.$$

The Kolmogorov scales satisfy \(\nu=u_\eta\,\eta\), and for isotropic turbulence the rms velocity is \(u'/u_\eta=\left(Re_\lambda/\sqrt{15}\right)^{1/2}\) (the isotropic relation \(\varepsilon=15\nu u'^2/\lambda^2\)). All radii are in units of \(\eta\), all velocities in units of \(u_\eta\) (written \(U_\eta\) in the model), and \(\Gamma\) in units of \(\nu\).

A0b. Physical equivalents of the DNS rows

A DNS is solved in dimensionless variables, \(\tilde x=x/\ell_0\), \(\tilde u=u/u_0\), \(\tilde t=t\,u_0/\ell_0\), \(\tilde\nu=\nu/(u_0\ell_0)\), \(\tilde\varepsilon=\varepsilon\,\ell_0/u_0^3\). Ghira et al. fix the box at \(2\pi\times2\pi\times2\pi\) (so \(\ell_0=L_{\text{box}}/2\pi\) and the Fourier wavenumbers are integers) and impose a random body force, delta-correlated in time and concentrated at the wavenumbers around \(k_p=2\) (Alvelius's scheme), whose mean power \(P=\langle f_iu_i\rangle=10\) is the same in every run. There are no walls and no mean flow; at steady state the viscous dissipation balances the power, so \(\tilde\varepsilon\approx10\) (Table II: 9.5–11.9) and the integral length \(\tilde L_{11}\approx0.85\) is set by the forcing. Only \(\tilde\nu\) changes between the runs (0.0145 to 0.0010), and it sets \(Re_\lambda\); the Kolmogorov length \(\tilde\eta=(\tilde\nu^3/\tilde\varepsilon)^{1/4}\) is the value listed in Table II (0.0235 for the first run). One series is therefore the same stirrer in fluids of falling viscosity.

To compare with a physical flow with kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) and dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\), the two scales \(\ell_0\) and \(u_0\) follow from two equations, \(\varepsilon=\tilde\varepsilon\,u_0^3/\ell_0\) and \(\nu=\tilde\nu\,u_0\ell_0\):

$$\ell_0=\frac{\eta_{\text{phys}}}{\tilde\eta}=\left(\frac{\nu^3/\varepsilon}{\tilde\nu^3/\tilde\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4},\qquad u_0=\frac{\nu}{\tilde\nu\,\ell_0},\qquad \eta_{\text{phys}}=\left(\frac{\nu^3}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4}.$$

The Reynolds number \(Re_\lambda\) is already used (through \(\tilde\nu\)), so one of \(\varepsilon\), \(\ell_0\) or \(u_0\) must be given besides \(\nu\). The model takes \(\varepsilon\) as given, as in section 7, and the reference used here is water, \(\nu=10^{-6}\) m\(^2\)/s, at \(\varepsilon=0.1\) W/kg, so \(\eta_{\text{phys}}=56.2\ \mu\)m. The results of the DNS in Kolmogorov units hold for any \(\varepsilon\); a different reference only rescales the lengths, and an \(r_{\max}\) of \(\beta\,\eta\) becomes \(\beta\times56.2\ \mu\)m. A single wire or crossed wire records one velocity component along a straight path through the flow. $$S(s)=\frac{v_\theta(\rho)}{\rho}\;\mathbf e_y\cdot\left(\mathbf a\times\mathbf p(s)\right).$$

Why the intense hot-wire classes are left out

The classes of Mouri et al. (2004, 2007) are the highest 0.1% and 1% of the absolute velocity increments of the same experiments as Mouri and Hori (2009); Fig. 2 of the latter shows that they are the tail of the distribution of the increments that the all-tube average also contains. The events above a high threshold are mostly crossings close to the axis, so their radii (5.2–7.4 \(\eta\)) are close to the true mean, as the authors write, and their swirl (4–14 \(U_\eta\)) is closer to the true swirl than that of the all-tube average. Two readings of the difference between the two values are possible: a chord distribution (the same tubes crossed at different offsets and tilts, as in Belin et al.) or a real difference in tube strength (the authors attribute the difference of V0 mainly to the true mean circulation velocity). The data cannot separate them. If it is the chord alone, the 0.1% row is a lower bound for the true swirl, which gives the all-tube swirl a factor of at least 3.1 at \(Re_\lambda=332\) and 4.4 at 1934; the factor 3 used is at the lower end of that bound. The tube that the chord model gives from the pair of rows depends on the tilt limit (swirl 10.9, 10.8 and 13.9 \(U_\eta\) at \(Re_\lambda=332\) for 20, 45 and 70°) and on whether the cut acts on the peak or on the increment (the ratio of the two cuts is 1.8 for the peak and 2.8 for the increment, against 1.86 read from the distribution of the increments in their Fig. 2), so the rows are not used as a calibration and are shown in A8 and A9 for comparison.
